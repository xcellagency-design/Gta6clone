# Performance

Measured on 2026-11-26 on the development machine: a cloud container with an Intel Xeon at
2.3 GHz (4 vCPU), 16 GB RAM and **no GPU**. Raw results are regenerated by:

```sh
npx tsx tools/bench_sim.mts     # → out/bench-sim.json
node tools/bench.mjs low medium # → out/bench-browser.json   (after npm run build)
```

## What can and cannot be concluded

- **CPU-side numbers are real** and transfer to other machines of similar speed: world
  generation, the simulation step, streaming work, memory.
- **Frame rates below are software rendering** (Chromium's SwiftShader; the WebGL renderer string
  is recorded in the results). Every pixel is shaded on the CPU. They show the game runs and
  where the cost is; they say nothing about frame rate on a GPU.
- **The 60 fps target is therefore unverified.** GPU utilisation and VRAM could not be measured.
  The workload a GPU would face is modest — see draw calls and triangles below — but that is an
  argument, not a measurement.

## Streaming under speed (Node)

A sports car driven flat out down the I-9, 2.7 km in 55 s, reaching 201 km/h, with generation
limited to two cells per frame:

- Frames in which play was held waiting for the world: **0**
- Cells loaded 1 604, unloaded 257; 766 resident at the end
- Streaming work per frame: 0.37 ms mean, 1.34 ms p99, 4.08 ms worst
- Resident cell data 10 MB; cache 31 MB (1 521 entries)

The unit suite repeats this at 162 km/h across the whole city with an asynchronous executor and
asserts the ground under the vehicle is always at full detail (`tests/unit/streaming.test.ts`).

Reading it: the simulation and streaming together cost about 1 ms a frame; the remaining 65–250
ms is the software rasteriser. The cost scales with pixels and shading, not with game logic.

### Streaming latency

Average time from a cell being wanted to being on screen was 2.5 s (low) and 24.7 s (medium) in
these runs. That figure is dominated by the frame rate: instantiation is budgeted at 4 ms *per
frame*, and at 4 fps there are few frames. Cells are served nearest and most-ahead first, so the
long tail is the far LOD3 ring. Worker dispatch was decoupled from the frame loop after the
first measurement; instantiation still is frame-bound by design.

## Bottlenecks found by profiling, and what was done

| Finding | Action |
|---|---|
| Far LOD3 ring cost two draw calls per cell | LOD3 cells are generated as a single mesh |
| Per-pixel noise from hashing interpolated values | Style parameters made `flat`; derivatives taken outside branches |
| Workers only received new jobs once per frame | Dispatch also runs when a worker finishes |
| Junction conflicts caused traffic overlaps | Junction reservation and lane discipline |

## Known performance risks

- A 316 ms frame was recorded once while driving on *low*. Likely a first-use shader compile or
  garbage collection; it was not isolated under software rendering.
- Every cell layer has its own material instance (for independent cross-fades). They share one
  compiled program, but uniform uploads grow with resident cells.
- Characters are 13 instances each; `high` can draw about 1 100 capsule instances.
- There is no occlusion culling; downtown overdraw is the worst case for a real GPU.
